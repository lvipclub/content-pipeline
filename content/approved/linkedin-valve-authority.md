**Valve Authority: The Forgotten Number in Hydronic Design**

Every commercial building has one: a chilled water loop that was never quite right. The coils near the plant are overfed, the far end runs lukewarm, and the pump — the most expensive component in the plant room — works hardest precisely when the system needs it least. The root causes are rarely mechanical failures. They are specification decisions made at the design stage, and they compound for the life of the asset.

**Why valve authority is the first number to check**

Valve authority (N) is the ratio of a control valve's pressure drop at design flow to the total pressure drop of the circuit it controls. It determines whether the valve's characteristic actually delivers what the control loop expects. Below approximately 0.3, the installed characteristic flattens: a valve that should give fine modulating control becomes effectively on-off, and the space temperature oscillates around the setpoint instead of holding it [ASHRAE Handbook — HVAC Systems and Equipment]. In retrofit audits, oversized control valves with authority in the 0.1-0.2 range are one of the most common findings — and one of the cheapest to correct at design stage, where the fix is a schedule change rather than a plant-room modification.

**Balancing: the step that makes the numbers true**

Hydronic circuits are inherently prone to the path-of-least-resistance problem. Without proportional balancing, the differential pressure across each coil is not what the designer calculated, and the control valves spend their lives fighting a distribution error rather than serving the load [CIBSE Guide B]. Balancing is not a commissioning nicety; it is the verification step that makes valve authority, coil performance and pump selection all true at the same time. Buildings that skip it discover the error in the first summer, when the tenant on the top floor files the first comfort complaint.

**The pump pays for all of it**

Pump energy follows the affinity laws: halving speed reduces power to approximately one-eighth. Variable-speed drives are now standard, but a drive cannot save energy the system never lets it use — an unbalanced loop holds the pump at higher speed than the load requires. Monitoring pump efficiency from BAS data (kW per unit flow at part load) turns the pump into a live diagnostic of the entire water-side system [ASHRAE Guideline 22].

**What this means for your next project**

The pattern across recent audits is consistent: the buildings that perform are not the ones with the most sophisticated controls — they are the ones whose water-side basics were specified correctly and verified at commissioning. Valve authority in the schedule, balancing as a documented step, and a pump that is measured, not assumed.
