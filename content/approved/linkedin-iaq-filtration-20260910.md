**Beyond MERV 13: Specifying Filtration for the Era of Smoke and Haze Episodes**

Indoor air quality is no longer a maintenance checkbox — it has shifted to an operational resilience metric, and it increasingly features in how tenants and asset managers judge a building. At the centre of that shift is fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and the question of how, precisely, to specify filtration for it.

The industry has largely settled on MERV 13 as the working baseline for commercial buildings, on the back of post-pandemic ASHRAE guidance. But rating alone is not a strategy. ISO 16890's ePM1 classification looks specifically at particles under one micron — the combustion byproducts and smoke particles that less efficient media let through — and high-pollution regions are increasingly specifying MERV 14 or ISO ePM1 80% performance in place of a bare MERV number.

There is a physics problem underneath all of this. As filtration efficiency rises, so does static pressure drop. Denser media require more fan energy to hold design airflow, and if the air handling unit was not sized with that headroom, the energy penalty lands on operating cost. We see this as the filtration-energy paradox: a poorly framed upgrade to high-efficiency media can quietly inflate fan energy and undermine the sustainability case that justified it. The practical answer is a whole-system approach — specify efficiency at a stated maximum initial pressure drop, confirm the fan and VFD headroom to carry the load as the filter loads up, and move filter change-outs to condition-based triggers based on differential-pressure data rather than a fixed calendar.

Acute pollution events change the strategy again. During smoke or haze episodes, the instinct to maximise outdoor air for dilution becomes counterproductive — it pumps pollution into the building. The better response is to lean on filtered recirculation while minimum ventilation rates are maintained, with a building automation system that responds to real-time outdoor PM2.5 data and adjusts the economiser and filtration staging accordingly.

For consultants and facility managers, the specification checklist is short:

- Specify performance, not just a rating — a target efficiency at a defined maximum initial pressure drop.
- Model the energy impact — lifecycle cost, not just first cost.
- Integrate IAQ into BAS logic — control sequences that react to outdoor particulate levels.
- Adopt condition-based maintenance — differential-pressure monitoring across filter banks.

A proactive, engineered approach to particulate control is now a core element of modern building management — impacting occupant health, compliance and long-term asset value.
