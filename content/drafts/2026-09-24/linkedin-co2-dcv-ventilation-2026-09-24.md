**Ventilation That Follows Occupancy: Making CO₂-Driven DCV Deliver**

Most commercial buildings still ventilate to a schedule written for a population that no longer sits in them. Fixed outdoor-air rates over-serve empty zones and under-serve full ones — paying conditioning energy for air nobody breathes, while crowded rooms drift past the conditions the design promised.

CO₂-based demand-controlled ventilation (DCV) replaces the schedule with a measurement. Because indoor CO₂ tracks occupant density and per-person outdoor air, it is the practical proxy a controller can reset against. ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 62.1 formalises this through DCV provisions that reset zone ventilation from a CO₂ band derived from the per-person outdoor-air rate [ANSI/ASHRAE Standard 62.1]. EN 16798-1 expresses the same logic as building categories: CO₂ elevation above outdoor levels [EN 16798-1].

The engineering lives in the details:

1. Derive the setpoint per zone — from the per-person rate and the zone's actual occupancy profile, not from a round number copied between projects.

2. The sensor is the system. NDIR sensors drift with age and environment. A DCV project without a calibration plan and a deliberate placement decision (breathing zone, or return duct only where the design accounts for it) will quietly measure something other than occupancy.

3. Floors, not ceilings. Reset never drops below code minimum outdoor air — and in humid climates, less outdoor air cuts latent load; it is not licence to starve the zone.

4. Verify. Trend CO₂ distributions and outdoor-air fractions against occupancy surveys — the DCV version of measurement and verification.

The case is not only energy. Field research has associated lower indoor CO₂ in office-like settings with measurably better cognitive performance [Allen et al., Environmental Health Perspectives, 2016]. For schools and open-plan offices — the two building types with the widest occupancy swings — DCV is where the health case and the energy case agree.
