# Topic: Energy efficiency — building energy codes (ASHRAE 90.1), retrofit savings, sub-metering & monitoring-based commissioning
# Date: Sat 08 Aug 2026

**Audience:** Specifiers, consultants, facility managers — market intelligence voice

**Hook:**

Energy codes across Asia are tightening faster than most specification cycles can keep up. Every edition of ASHRAE 90.1 since 2004 has cut the modelled energy use of commercial buildings, and jurisdictions from Singapore to Shenzhen now adopt code minima as their own baseline — some ahead of the US schedule. The building you specify this year will be measured against rules written for the decade it actually operates in.

Three shifts separate the projects that will age well from the ones that will be retrofitted at someone else's expense:

1. **Codes compound; they don't plateau.** Each 90.1 revision tightens HVAC minimum efficiencies, fan and pump power limits, and controls requirements — the 2019 edition alone cut modelled energy use by roughly 8.5% against 2016, and the 2022 edition tightens further [US DOE determination for ASHRAE 90.1-2019]. A spec that meets "today's code" with no margin is obsolete before the building opens.

2. **Sub-metering is becoming the default, not the upgrade.** More jurisdictions are moving toward mandatory whole-building and end-use metering so that performance is verifiable rather than assumed. A building that cannot show where its energy goes cannot prove compliance — and increasingly cannot secure green certification or preferred financing terms.

3. **Monitoring-based commissioning (MBCx) is the retrofit that pays for itself.** Persistent analytics on BAS data — comparing actual vs expected operation, flagging economiser faults, drifted sensors and scheduling overrides — typically delivers whole-building savings in the 5–15% range with paybacks under two years [Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, commissioning meta-analysis]. For existing buildings it is the cheapest kilowatt-hour available.

What this means for your next spec: design to the next code edition, not the current one; put sub-metering points in the controls schedule; and write a monitoring-based commissioning clause into the O&M handover. The buildings being designed today will be judged on measured performance — not on the brochure.
