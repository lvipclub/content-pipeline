# Topic: Water-side — control valve selection, valve authority, hydronic balancing & pump efficiency
# Date: Thu 06 Aug 2026

**Audience:** Specifiers, consultants, facility managers — market intelligence voice

**Hook:**

The water-side is where the biggest silent energy losses in commercial HVAC live — and they are almost always specified into existence. A control valve chosen by pipe size instead of authority, a hydronic loop never balanced, a pump sized for the worst-case never realised: each one is a small line-item that compounds into kW and comfort complaints for the life of the building.

Three specification decisions separate a hydronic system that controls itself from one that fights itself for decades:

1. **Valve authority is a spec number, not a corner case.** Authority (N) is the ratio of the valve's pressure drop at design flow to the total pressure drop of the circuit it controls. Below roughly 0.3 the valve's characteristic flattens and the loop loses controllability — the classic oversized-valve failure where a 2°C adjustment becomes a 6°C swing. A spec that fixes the authority range (0.3 minimum, 0.5 typical) before the valve is selected is the cheapest insurance in hydronic design [ASHRAE Handbook — HVAC Systems and Equipment].

2. **Balancing is what makes the numbers true.** Without proportional balancing, water takes the path of least resistance — the coils nearest the plant are overfed while the far end of the building runs lukewarm. That is not a control fault; it is a distribution fault written into the design. Specify balancing valves with measured setpoints and a commissioning record, not a hope [CIBSE Guide B].

3. **The pump pays for all of it.** Pump power follows the affinity laws — halving speed cuts power to roughly one-eighth. An un-balanced system forces the pump to push against closed authority, wasting the exact energy that variable-speed drives were meant to save. Measure pump efficiency from BAS data (kW per L/s at part load) and you will see the whole system's health in one number.

What this means for your next spec: put the authority calculation in the valve schedule, make balancing a documented commissioning step, and give the pump a variable-speed drive with a monitoring point. The difference between a plant room that hums and one that screams is three lines in the specification.
