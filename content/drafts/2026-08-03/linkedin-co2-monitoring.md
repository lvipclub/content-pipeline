## CO₂ Sensors Are Cheap. Bad Placement Is Expensive.

The commercial building sector has discovered CO₂ monitoring — and is now discovering that a sensor's number is only as good as its installation. Across recent projects in Singapore, Hong Kong, and the Gulf, we keep seeing the same pattern: demand-controlled ventilation installed with confidence, and then years of arguments between facilities and engineering about readings that drift 200-300 ppm apart between two sensors in the same room.

The engineering reality is well understood. The specification gap is not.

**The technology is sound — the assumptions are not.** Modern non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) sensors are stable and accurate, typically ±50 ppm + 5% of reading. But their self-calibration routines (automatic baseline correction) assume a space periodically sees near-outdoor air, around 400 ppm. Spaces that rarely do — sealed meeting rooms, low-occupancy floors outside peak hours — drift upward over months. The BMS responds by over-ventilating, which quietly converts the intended energy saving into fan and thermal load [ASHRAE 62.1].

**Placement is a specification decision, not an installation afterthought.** A sensor mounted beside a supply diffuser reads diluted supply air. One above a doorway reads the corridor. ASHRAE 62.1's guidance points to the breathing zone, or to a return airstream that genuinely represents the occupied-zone average. When the placement is left to site conditions, the reading represents whatever happens to be near the sensor — not what occupants experience [ASHRAE 62.1-2022, Informative Appendix].

**Verification turns a sensor into an instrument.** A reference-gas check at commissioning, then at the manufacturer's interval, is inexpensive and transforms the sensor from an unverifiable black box into a defensible measurement. Budgets rarely include it; disputes usually follow.

**The honest limitation.** CO₂ is an excellent proxy for occupancy-driven ventilation demand — the 62.1 ventilation rate procedure uses it that way. It is not a universal air-quality gauge: it does not detect VOC off-gassing from fit-out materials, PM2.5 ingress, or formaldehyde. Projects that treat CO₂ as the whole IAQ story will be surprised when the other pollutants show up. A complete IAQ strategy pairs CO₂-based DCV with the ventilation rates that match the space's actual pollutant sources [ASHRAE 62.1].

**What this means for your next project.** Four lines in the specification: sensor accuracy class (NDIR, ±50 ppm), placement per the breathing-zone rule, a commissioning baseline, and a recalibration interval. The difference between a DCV project that delivers its promised outdoor-air energy savings and one that argues about readings for years is that small.
